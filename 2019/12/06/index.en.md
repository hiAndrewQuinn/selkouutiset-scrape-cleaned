# Friday 6.12.2019 (radio)

Finland has celebrated Independence Day. The Presidential Palace's Independence Day reception turns 100 years old. Only basic comprehensive school is most common among older age groups. The Sadankomitea peace prize goes to Yle News foreign correspondent Antti Kuronen.

## Independence Day of 102-year-old Finland

![Suomalaisuuden Liitto's national flag-raising at Tähtitorninmäki in Helsinki on 6 December 2019.](https://img.img-cdn.yle.fi/crop_extract,w_5345,h_3007,x_0,y_551/crop_fill,w_767,h_431,ar_16:9,dpr_1/f_auto/39-6198605dea014610a30/1575616994)

Finland has celebrated Independence Day. The day traditionally began with a flag-raising at Tähtitorninmäki in Helsinki.

The Defence Forces' Independence Day parade was in Tampere. About 30,000 people watched the parade, even though the weather was rainy.

A special feature of the parade was a large cavalry unit, which mainly had Finnhorses.

The Independence Day reception is held at the Presidential Palace. Veterans of the wars and Lottas are the guests of honour at the party.

## The palace parties have a long tradition

![President Relander shakes hands with Theodor Schauman, and Mrs Relander with Alma Schauman. Behind them, on the left, is Heikki Kekoni and, on the right, Börje Söderström.](https://img.img-cdn.yle.fi/crop_fill,w_767,h_431,dpr_1/f_auto/13-3-11095327)

The Presidential Palace's Independence Day party turns 100 years old.

The party was first held on 6 December 1919. President K. J. Ståhlberg invited the government, civil servants and diplomats to the palace.

The reception was in the afternoon. There were only about 150 guests, and the party lasted one hour. Nowadays the palace is full of guests, and the party lasts late into the evening.

Dancing became part of the party in 1925.

During the war years of 1939–1945, the party was held only once, in 1943. That time the party was at the Turku VPK, that is, the volunteer fire brigade's building.

A live TV broadcast was first shown in 1957. Between 1969 and 1980 the party was not shown live on TV. At that time, people did not want to show how the elite celebrates.

Nowadays the guests' handshakes are the most watched part of the party. Any viewer can test how many public figures they recognise. The handshakes are also a challenge for TV commentators.

## Only basic school is enough for few jobs

![Desks from Oinaa School](https://img.img-cdn.yle.fi/crop_fill,w_767,h_431,dpr_1/f_auto/13-3-9083602)

Open image viewer

Old school desks are being collected for a school museum. Photo: Jorma Korhonen / Yle

Some Finns have only completed basic school, or its predecessor, the folk school.

Only folk school or basic school is most common among older age groups. Only basic school is not rare among younger adults either.

The year before last, 16 per cent of Finns aged 20 to 29 had only basic school. Over the last 30 years, more and more people have also studied after basic school.

Nowadays there are fewer and fewer jobs that only require basic school. Many jobs require some qualification.

## Sadankomitea honours Yle's Antti Kuronen

![Antti Kuronen in Qamishli, Syria, 25 May 2019.](https://img.img-cdn.yle.fi/crop_fill,w_767,h_431,dpr_1/f_auto/13-3-11106512)

Yle News foreign correspondent Antti Kuronen. Photo: Yle

The peace organisation Sadankomitea's peace prize this year goes to Yle News foreign correspondent Antti Kuronen. The reason for the award is his excellent journalism in conflict areas.

Sadankomitea wants to highlight the role of the media in peace work. The award also reminds us how important fact-based information is.

In recent years, Yle's Antti Kuronen has reported mainly on the wars in Syria, Ukraine and Gaza.
